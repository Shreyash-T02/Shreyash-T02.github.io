### The problem

Powered descent and landing is one of the few control problems with no opportunity to retry. The vehicle has to null out horizontal drift, manage a rapidly changing mass as propellant burns off, and touch down inside a tight envelope of vertical rate, lateral velocity, and tilt angle — all while thrust authority is limited and the surface underneath may not be level.

**Leapfrog** is a terrestrial prototype that lets this regime be studied experimentally: a thrust-controlled vehicle that lifts, translates, and lands under its own propulsion, standing in for a lunar lander without leaving the ground.

### What I do

I work with the **hardware and propulsion teams**, sitting between the test campaign and the design cycle:

- **Analyse test results** from the propulsion and vehicle hardware, turning raw run data into statements about what the vehicle actually did versus what it was commanded to do.
- **Propose design iterations that improve landing stability** — the touchdown phase is where small asymmetries in leg loading, tilt, or residual lateral rate compound into failure.
- **Support propulsion hardware integration**, keeping the physical build consistent with the assumptions the analysis depends on.

### What I take from it

Test data is unsentimental. A landing either stayed inside the envelope or it did not, and the log tells you which — the interesting work is explaining *why*, then changing the hardware so the next run is different. That loop, from instrumented test back into mechanical design, is the same one I ran on Formula Student data acquisition, and it is the fastest way I know to find where a model is lying.
